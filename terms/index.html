5. Accuracy and limits of the model

5.1 Results are estimates from a simplified model. Sketch FEA calculates with a simplified two-dimensional model, the finite element method with quadratic triangular elements. A plane stress model is a thin plate of a set thickness. A plane strain model is a slice of a long part that cannot stretch along its length. An axisymmetric model is a round part whose loads and supports are the same all the way around. Real parts are three-dimensional, so results can differ from real behavior by a large amount.

5.2 What the model leaves out or simplifies.

  • Three-dimensional behavior.
  • Large deflections and large strains. Results are not reliable once the largest deflection passes about 5% of the part’s size, or strains pass a few percent outside Breakage; the app warns about large deflections.
  • Contact and friction. Parts that touch are either joined or separate.
  • Materials. Each material is isotropic and linear elastic, and its properties do not change with temperature. Composites and other materials whose properties depend on direction are not modeled; wood is shown as an isotropic approximation.
  • Material values. The library holds typical values from published sources. Real parts vary with the grade, the supplier and the manufacturing process, 3D-printed parts especially. Some values have no checked source and are shown as “not set”, and a material without them cannot be used for the analyses that need them.
  • Singular points. At a sharp inside corner, at the end of a support, and under a point load or a point support, the stress is not finite in theory. The value shown there depends on the mesh and keeps rising as the mesh gets finer.
  • The mesh. Every result is an approximation on a mesh of a set fineness. The app shows an estimate of the error, which is itself an estimate.
  • Strength. The factor of safety compares the stress with a typical yield or tensile strength. It does not include fatigue, creep, corrosion, wear, residual stress, welds, bolt preload, manufacturing defects or surface finish.
  • Buckling. The buckling load is that of a perfect part. Real parts, with their small flaws, can buckle at a lower load.
  • Dynamics. Damping is a value you set, not a calculated one.
  • Breakage. Yielding, cracking and breaking follow simplified material curves with default values. The peak force matches the tensile strength by construction; the elongation at break and the crack’s path are estimates, and necking is not modeled. A brittle material without a checked fracture toughness, such as the library’s glass, cracks open at once.

Help and shortcuts > Model in the app describes the model in more detail.

5.3 Verified against exact solutions and benchmark cases, not validated against the real world. The calculation has been verified against published exact solutions and benchmark cases. Its results were compared with published values, and each comparison had to fall within a set tolerance: 0.5% for exact displacements, energies, frequencies, temperatures and heat flows; 1% for peak stresses on curved edges; 2% for published formulas that are themselves approximations, and for peaks in time; 3% for limit loads and peak forces in breakage. The cases are:

  • beams: a cantilever under an end load and a simply supported beam under a uniform load, with the exact solutions of the theory of elasticity: Timoshenko, S. P. and Goodier, J. N. (1951), Theory of Elasticity;
  • a hole in a plate: Kirsch, E. G. (1898), Zeitschrift des Vereines deutscher Ingenieure 42, 797–807; and in a strip of finite width: Howland, R. C. J. (1930), Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society of London A 229, 49–86;
  • a thick-walled cylinder under pressure: Lamé, G. (1852), Leçons sur la théorie mathématique de l’élasticité des corps solides;
  • a rotating disk, a curved bar in bending, and thermal stress in a held bar and a thick cylinder: Timoshenko and Goodier (1951);
  • the stress at a sharp inside corner: Williams, M. L. (1952), Journal of Applied Mechanics 19, 526–528;
  • heat conduction through walls and cylinders, a cooling fin, a suddenly heated block and a slab cooled by convection: Lienhard, J. H. V and Lienhard, J. H. IV (2024), A Heat Transfer Textbook, 6th ed.;
  • a bimetal strip: Timoshenko, S. (1925), Journal of the Optical Society of America 11, 233–255;
  • the natural frequencies of a cantilever: Timoshenko, S. P. (1921), Philosophical Magazine 41, 744–746, and Cowper, G. R. (1966), Journal of Applied Mechanics 33, 335–340;
  • the buckling load of a column: Euler, L. (1744), and Timoshenko, S. P. and Gere, J. M. (1961), Theory of Elastic Stability;
  • yielding of a bar and of a thick cylinder: Simo, J. C. and Hughes, T. J. R. (1998), Computational Inelasticity, and Hill, R. (1950), The Mathematical Theory of Plasticity;
  • the energy of a brittle crack: Bažant, Z. P. and Oh, B. H. (1983), Matériaux et Constructions 16, 155–177;
  • patch tests that each element must pass exactly, and the balance of forces and of heat in every case.

These checks show that the model’s equations are solved as intended for those cases. They do not show that the model represents any real part, structure or product. Sketch FEA has not been validated against measurements, physical tests or certified engineering software, and it has not been tested in real-world scenarios. The checks were run on a computer with the same calculation code, which gives identical results in the JavaScript engine of iPhone and iPad.
